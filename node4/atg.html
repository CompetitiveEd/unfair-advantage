What she inherited — the problems

Blackstone bought NEC Group in 2018 for ~£800m. By the time Smith arrived in April 2022, COVID had gutted the business. 55% of the workforce had been cut. Glassdoor was 2.5 out of 5. The venue was trying to do everything itself — run catering through Amadeus (its own in-house operation), run ticketing through The Ticket Factory (its own platform), manage every aspect of venue operations. All of it mediocre. None of it best-in-class.

Exhibitors were threatening to move to ExCeL London and Olympia — where visitors could actually use their phones, eat decent food, and buy tickets on a mobile app. NEC had no Wi-Fi worth using, no mobile signal in large parts of the campus, no data on who was visiting, and no way to upsell or personalise. Seven million people came through the doors every year and NEC knew almost nothing about any of them.

The Blackstone thesis was simple: buy the asset, fix the operations, improve the EBITDA, exit at a premium. Smith's job was to turn a £800m purchase into a £1bn+ exit in 3 years. That meant EBITDA had to grow fast and the growth had to be structural, not cosmetic.

The Ticket Factory was behind every competitor. Staff were spread too thin across too many functions. Decision: stop doing everything. Return to the landlord model. Focus on what NEC is actually good at — operating the physical venue. Everything else goes to a specialist who does it better.

The business logic

Every decision traces back to one insight: the customer experience was broken, and that's why revenue was stuck. Exhibitors were leaving because visitors hated coming. Visitors hated coming because the food was bad, the signal was dead, the ticketing was clunky, and the venue felt like 1995. Fix the experience and the revenue follows. But NEC couldn't fix it by doing everything itself — it didn't have the people, the skills, or the money to be world-class at catering AND ticketing AND technology AND venue operations. So she outsourced everything that wasn't core, invested the savings into the things that matter to the customer, and let the specialists do what they do best.

Result: revenue to £190m, EBITDA up 33%, Blackstone targeting £1bn+ exit at ~18x EV/EBITDA (paid £800m). All in 2.5 years.

The model — vertical alignment without vertical integration

How Providence operates — and why it matters

Providence doesn't build conglomerates. They build ecosystems. Each company is independent — separate P&L, separate management team, separate exit timeline. There is no group overhead, no shared services centre, no group CTO. But the portfolio creates natural gravity. Theatre companies need staging companies. Exhibition businesses need IT infrastructure. Live events need logistics. Every pound spent within the ecosystem improves EBITDA at two entities simultaneously.

Providence doesn't consolidate the companies into one group P&L. Each company is a separate exit — different buyers, different multiples, different timelines. All the benefit of vertical integration, none of the cost. The integration happens at the portfolio level, not the company level.

Think of it like a regulated utility group — National Grid owning separate regulated entities — but without the regulatory overhead. No transfer pricing scrutiny. No regulator capping returns. Just a smart PE owner who knows that if you put the best theatre operator, the best staging company, the best logistics firm, and the best IT services provider in the same stable, good things happen naturally. Each business grows independently, becomes the best in its market, keeps its own EBITDA clean, and creates compound value across the portfolio.

The critical rule — non-Providence revenue must be the majority

If 80% of a portfolio company's revenue comes from other Providence companies, it's a captive supplier trading at a captive multiple. If 85% is external, it's a market leader with warm relationships. Exit story completely different. Every company must win in the open market first. The Providence relationships are a bonus, not the business.

A buyer doing due diligence will ask: "what percentage of revenue comes from other Providence companies?" If the answer is too high, the buyer discounts the multiple because the revenue isn't durable post-exit. Providence knows this. That's why they want each company to compete externally and win.
